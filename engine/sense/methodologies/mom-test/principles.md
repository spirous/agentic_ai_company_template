# The Mom Test — Working Methodology

Distilled from *The Mom Test* by Rob Fitzpatrick. Full book: `shared/references/The-Mom-Test-by-@robfitz.pdf`.

Core premise: everyone lies to you about your idea, especially people who like you. The fix is not to ask better people. It is to ask questions that even your mom couldn't lie to you about.

## The three rules
1. **Talk about their life instead of your idea.** The conversation is about the customer's problems, workflows, and money, not your solution.
2. **Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future.** Past behavior is evidence. Predictions and opinions are noise.
3. **Talk less and listen more.** Every second you spend pitching is a second you are not learning.

The measure of a question: does it still yield useful information if the person never learns what you are building?

## Good questions vs bad questions
| Bad question | Good replacement | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "Do you think it's a good idea?" | "How do you currently deal with X? Talk me through the last time it happened." | Opinions are worthless. Only the market knows. Workflow facts are evidence. |
| "Would you buy a product that did X?" | "What have you already tried to solve this? What did it cost you?" | Anyone will say yes to a hypothetical. If they never tried to solve it, they won't buy your fix. |
| "How much would you pay for X?" | "What does this problem cost you now? What are you currently paying to deal with it?" | People are bad at predicting spend. Current cost is a real price anchor. |
| "What would your dream product do?" | "Why do you want that? What would it let you do? How are you coping without it?" | Feature wishlists are only useful for the motivations underneath them. |
| "Do you have problem X?" (leading) | "What's the hardest part of doing Y?" | Leading questions harvest false positives. Open questions surface real pain ranking. |
| "Would you use a solution like this?" | "What are the implications of that problem?" | Separates must-solve problems from tolerable annoyances people won't pay to fix. |
| "This is important, right?" | "Why do you bother doing it that way?" | "Why do you bother" points at motivation and often reveals the problem behind the problem. |
| Pitching, then "so what do you think?" | "What else should I have asked?" | People want to help. Give them an opening to correct your blind spots. |
| Ending with "great, I'll keep you posted." | "Who else should I talk to?" | Every good conversation ends with intros. It also tests whether they actually care. |
| "Would your company pay for this?" (B2B) | "Where would the budget for this come from? Who else is involved in the decision?" | Maps the real buying process instead of one person's enthusiasm. |

Rule of thumb: when you hear a generic claim ("I always", "I usually", "I would"), anchor it: "When did that last happen? Walk me through it."

## Evidence classification rubric
| Type | Sounds like | Worth | What to do |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Facts** | Specific past behavior, workflows, numbers: "Last month we lost two days to this and paid a contractor 3k." | Gold. The core of learning. | Write down exact quotes. Dig for cost, frequency, workarounds, who was involved. |
| **Commitments** | Giving up time, reputation, or money: booking the next meeting, intro to their boss, a deposit. | The only proof they mean it. | Accept, confirm concretely, follow through. |
| **Compliments** | "That's really cool." "I love it." "Neat idea." | Worthless, fool's gold. Usually means you pitched. | Deflect ("thanks, but…") and return to their life and past specifics. Never write them down as validation. |
| **Fluff** | Generics ("I usually…"), hypotheticals ("I would…"), futures ("I'll definitely buy it when it launches"). | Zero. People are wildly optimistic about their future selves. | Anchor to specifics: "When was the last time? What did you do about it? What else did you try?" |
| **Ideas / feature requests** | "You should add X." "Can it integrate with Y?" | A signpost, not a spec. | Do not add to the roadmap. Dig beneath: "Why do you want that? How are you coping without it? Would the product still work for you without it?" |

## Commitment and advancement
A meeting succeeded only if it ended with advancement: a concrete step forward through the funnel. Compliments plus vague friendliness equal failure.

Currencies of commitment, weak to strong:
- **Time.** Weak: "happy to chat again sometime." Strong: a scheduled next meeting with a known agenda, sitting down to give detailed feedback on wireframes, using a trial seriously for a set period.
- **Reputation.** Weak: "I could probably introduce you to some people." Strong: an intro made this week to their boss, team, or budget owner, agreeing to be a public case study or testimonial.
- **Money.** Weak: "we'd probably pay for that." Strong: a letter of intent, a pre-order, a deposit, a signed pilot.

Zombie signals: "Sounds great, keep me posted." "Let me know when it launches." "I love it" with no next step. These are polite brush-offs. Counter by asking for a specific commitment now. If they dodge, they were never a lead, and that is useful data. It is better to get a clear no than to feed a zombie pipeline.

If someone is emotionally engaged but cannot buy (wrong role, no budget), still harvest intros and understanding, then move on.

## Red flags
- **Pitching instead of listening.** Symptom: you are doing most of the talking, they are complimenting you. You leaked your ego into the conversation.
- **Revealing your idea too early.** Once they know what you want to hear, every answer is polluted. Delay the reveal, or never reveal at all in early problem discovery.
- **Fishing for compliments.** Asking "do you like it?" in any form, then logging the yes as validation.
- **Counting wishy-washy meetings as wins.** "It went really well, they loved it" almost always means you got compliments and no commitment. Bad meetings feel good; good meetings produce facts and next steps.
- **Only talking to friendly audiences.** Friends, fans, and people who enjoy talking to founders will never give you the hard no you need.
- **Formality.** Turning every learning conversation into a scheduled "meeting" adds friction, raises stakes, and invites performance instead of honesty.
- **Founder as data bottleneck.** If one person attends all conversations and relays summaries, the team learns filtered conclusions, not evidence.

## Asking for and running the meeting
Cold and warm asks use the framing format, five pieces:
1. **Vision.** One sentence on the problem you are trying to fix, not your product. "I'm trying to make conference sponsorship less of a gamble for sponsors."
2. **Framing.** Set expectations and take selling off the table. "I'm not selling anything, I'm just trying to understand how this really works."
3. **Weakness.** Show how you are stuck, which gives them a way to help. "We keep hitting a wall understanding how budgets get approved."
4. **Pedestal.** Why them specifically. "You know more about this buying process than anyone I can reach."
5. **Ask.** Explicit and small. "Could you spare 30 minutes in the next week?"

People love to help and love being the expert. Ask for advice, never for a sales meeting. Warm intros through mutual contacts, communities, or existing customers beat cold outreach. Teaching, blogging, and showing up where your segment gathers create warm inbound.

Keep it casual. Early problem-discovery conversations do not need to be meetings at all. Five minutes at a conference or over coffee can settle whether a problem exists and matters. Length grows as you move toward product feedback and sales conversations. If it is not casual, make sure it ends in commitment.

## Segmentation
"Everyone" is not a customer segment, and averaging feedback across mixed segments produces mush. Customer slicing:
1. Start with your broad segment and ask: within this group, who wants this most? Who has the strongest motivation, the most acute pain, the budget?
2. For that subgroup, ask why they want it, and whether everyone in the subgroup shares that motivation. Slice again on the motivations.
3. Convert each slice into a **who-where pair**: a specific type of person plus a concrete place you can find them (a community, event, channel, job title, watering hole).
4. Pick the slice that is profitable, reachable, and personally rewarding to serve.
5. If you cannot say where to find them, the segment is still too fuzzy. Re-slice until it is findable.

Consistent, repeatable signals only appear once you talk to a coherent slice. Confusing feedback usually means a segmentation problem, not a product problem.

## Prep and review discipline
- **Know your 3 big questions before every conversation.** Choose the ones that could kill the business, the scary ones you are avoiding. If you do not know what you are trying to learn, do not have the conversation.
- Prep as a team: agree the current big 3, guess the answers in advance, and note which answer would change your behavior. A question whose answer changes nothing is not worth asking.
- Different conversations can carry different questions. Do not burn a rare senior contact on questions any customer could answer.
- **Take notes with exact quotes**, marked as quotes, plus specifics: numbers, names, tools, dates. Tag signals as you go: pain, goal, obstacle, workaround, feature request, budget, person to talk to, follow-up task, and emotion (excitement, anger, embarrassment). Emotions mark the spots worth digging into.
- Notes must be permanent, searchable, and shared. Data locked in one founder's head is lost.
- **Review with the team after each batch.** Transfer notes, update your beliefs, kill or promote hypotheses, and rewrite the big 3 for the next round. Bring a second person to important meetings when possible.

## One-page checklist
**Before**
- Segment chosen is specific and findable (who-where pair).
- Big 3 questions agreed, each one able to change a decision.
- You know what commitment or advancement you will ask for, if the stage warrants one.
- Ask framed as advice (vision, framing, weakness, pedestal, ask), kept casual.

**During**
- Talk about their life, not your idea. They talk more than you.
- Ask about specific past instances. Anchor every generic to "when did that last happen?"
- Deflect compliments. Do not reveal the idea early. Do not pitch.
- Dig beneath every feature request and strong emotion for the motivation.
- Note exact quotes and specifics: costs, frequency, tools, people, budget owner.
- Close with commitment or advancement, plus "who else should I talk to?" and "what should I have asked?"

**After**
- Was there any commitment of time, reputation, or money? If not, log it as a miss and diagnose why.
- Review notes the same day. Update beliefs and the big 3.
- Transfer notes to permanent shared storage. Follow through on every commitment made.
- If all you collected was compliments and fluff, fix your questions before the next conversation.
